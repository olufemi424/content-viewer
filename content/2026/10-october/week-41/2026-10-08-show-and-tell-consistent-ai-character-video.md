## Audience + difficulty


## Why now (1 sentence)
A finished 20–30 second vertical character video in which the same AI-created character appears in three different shots: a close-up, a full-body scene, and a moving final shot. The final screen shows the three clips playing in sequence with the character’s face, wardrobe anchor, and visual style staying recognizably consistent.

**Exact input:** one short creative brief, for example: “Create a 25-second vertical ad for a fictional coffee brand. Follow Maya, a warm, silver-haired barista, from opening the shop to serving a customer. Keep her face, red apron, and warm morning palette consistent across three shots.”

**Tool/action:** turn the brief into an approved character reference sheet and shot plan, then use a reference-aware image/video tool such as Google Flow, OpenArt, Kling, or a comparable image-to-video workflow to generate still keyframes and animate them.

**Output:** one record-ready vertical character video plus the reusable character sheet, shot prompts, and approved keyframes.

**First-three-seconds visual:** open on the completed three-shot video or a split-screen of the same character in all three scenes. Do not begin with a blank prompt box.

## Final record-ready script
Want to create a consistent AI character video from one brief? Let me show you how.

This is the final result: one short creative brief turned into a finished vertical video, with the same character appearing across three different scenes.

Step one: start with a small brief. Define the character, the action, the setting, the mood, and the format. For this example, it is a 25-second coffee ad in a vertical frame.

Step two: build the character reference before you make the video. Generate a clean character sheet with a front view, a three-quarter view, and a full-body view. Lock the face, hair, red apron, accessory, and color palette.

Do not change those identity details from shot to shot. Put them in a reusable character block. Only change the action, camera, location, or lighting.

Step three: create three still keyframes. One for the close-up, one for the full-body action, and one for the final movement. Approve the stills first. If the face or wardrobe is wrong now, it will not magically become right after animation.

Step four: animate one shot at a time. Use image-to-video or a reference mode, keep the motion simple, and reuse the approved image. Text-only prompts are much more likely to drift because they are rebuilding the character every time.

Step five: run a continuity check. Compare the face, hair, clothing, proportions, colors, and action. If one shot drifts, regenerate that shot—do not rewrite the whole character.

Here is the finishing detail: make a continuity lock. It is a short list of the details that cannot change. Keep it beside your keyframes while you review, then fix the opening frame so the character is recognizable immediately.
